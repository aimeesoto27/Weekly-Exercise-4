Civic technology is technology that is created using civic or public data to 
benefit the general public and/or solve a civic issue (David, et al., 2017). 
The main driver when it comes to civic technology and those who create it is the 
desire to better their communities and the public sector. Smart cities are 
governments that embrace these methods of innovation and continue the pillars of 
open and efficient governments. Smart cities are open to change and reforming
traditional government set ups in order to embrace innovation and unique
solutions utilizing technology. Civic iq (2026) defines how smart cities are 
considered any city that has a system to watch or control its physical assets. 
This definition less so embraces innovation and more so views technology as a 
security mechanism. This article researched where smart cities were investing 
their dollars and the most contracts came from the public safety and 
surveillance category of technology. Therefore, instead of being a progressive, 
technological driven utopia, smart cities may actually be highly technological 
surveillance states. This brings me to the topic of flock cameras and their 
recent divide within the nation. The New York Times (2026) published an article 
named, “Flock Cameras are Dividing U.S. Cities. Here’s What to Know About Them.” 
This article outlines flock cameras are automated license plate-reading cameras 
that are used by law enforcement agencies to detect crime. Flock cameras were 
created by a private company and are now being leased by public agencies to 
deter crime. However, these cameras pose civil liberty concerns as some citizens 
say they violate privacy protections and indicate a surveillance state. Overall, 
civic technology can be exciting and help create incredible solutions to everyday 
issues, however smart cities can very easily go awry if they’re too quick to 
innovate before properly considering the implications of the misuse of data.
